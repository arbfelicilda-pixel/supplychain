You Don't Know Your True Inventory Cost — And It's Draining Your Cash

By Arnie Rose FelicildaSupply Chain Management8 min read

The number on your purchase order is not what your inventory costs you. It is the starting price. By the time that product sits on your shelf, gets counted, insured, stored, and eventually sold — the real cost is 20 to 40 percent higher than what you paid for it.

Most operations managers know their purchase price down to the cent. Almost none can tell you their true inventory cost. That gap between what you think you are spending and what you are actually spending is the hidden drain that makes cash flow feel tighter than your sales numbers suggest it should be.

What True Inventory Cost Actually Includes

True inventory cost is the total cost of acquiring, holding, and managing a unit of inventory from the moment you order it to the moment it is sold. It is not just the unit price. It is everything attached to that unit from procurement to sale.

Cost Component 01

Purchase Price

What you paid the supplier per unit

Cost Component 02

Freight and Landed Cost

Shipping, duties, customs, and handling — often 10–25% of unit price

Cost Component 03

Storage and Warehousing

Allocated warehouse cost per SKU per month

Cost Component 04

Capital and Opportunity Cost

The return you could have earned on that money elsewhere — typically 8–12% annually

Cost Component 05

Insurance and Risk

Coverage cost allocated to your average inventory value

Cost Component 06

Obsolescence and Shrinkage

Estimated annual write-off as a percentage of total inventory value

The Real Number: A Worked Example

Take a product with a unit purchase price of $10.00. Here is what the true cost looks like once you add all components:

  • Purchase price: $10.00
  • Freight and duties (15%): $1.50
  • Warehousing allocation: $0.80
  • Capital cost (10% annually, 60-day hold): $0.16
  • Insurance allocation: $0.12
  • Obsolescence provision (5%): $0.50

True cost per unit: $13.08 — 31% above purchase price.

If you are pricing that product based on a $10 cost, your margins are 31% thinner than you think. If you are making inventory decisions based on a $10 cost, you are systematically underestimating the value of moving that product faster.

This is not an edge case. On a $100,000 inventory position with a 30% true cost markup, you are carrying $30,000 in costs that most managers never see in a single line item. It is spread across rent, insurance, write-offs, and finance costs — invisible until you calculate it deliberately.

Why Most Managers Never Calculate True Inventory Cost

The honest answer is that most accounting systems are not set up to show it. Purchase price lives in the PO system. Freight lives in logistics. Warehousing lives in facilities. Insurance lives in finance. Obsolescence lives in write-offs at year end.

No single report pulls all six components together for a per-SKU true cost calculation. So most managers work with the number that is easiest to find — purchase price — and make every subsequent decision on incomplete data.

How True Inventory Cost Changes Your Decisions

Pricing Decisions

If your margins are calculated on purchase price, your actual margins are lower. Knowing true cost gives you an accurate picture of profitability by SKU — which items are genuinely profitable and which ones look profitable but are not once all costs are included.

Ordering Decisions

When you know what it costs to hold inventory, the decision to order more becomes more deliberate. The carrying cost of an extra 60 days of stock is no longer invisible — it is a number you can weigh against the cost of a potential stockout.

Supplier Negotiations

True landed cost is the right number to use when comparing suppliers. A supplier with a lower unit price but higher lead time may have a higher true cost once freight, capital cost, and safety stock requirements are factored in. See the Total Landed Cost Calculator for a complete comparison framework.

SKU Rationalization

When you know the true cost of carrying each SKU, the decision to discontinue low-performers becomes financially clear. A C item with a true carrying cost of $2,000 per year that generates $1,500 in annual gross profit is a loss — even if the purchase price math looks positive.

Calculate your true inventory cost this week. Pick your five highest-volume SKUs. Add up all six cost components. Compare the result to your current pricing and margin assumptions. The gap you find is the number that explains why cash flow feels tighter than your revenue suggests it should.
